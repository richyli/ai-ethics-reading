In November 2026, Anthropic, the company behind Claude, filed the third amendment to its S-1 registration statement with the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) ahead of going public. The "Risk Factors" section of a prospectus is written for investors under legal liability: misstating or omitting a material risk can expose the company to lawsuits later. That makes it different from a press release or blog post, and a good source for what an AI company formally acknowledges. This filing's Risk Factors begin on page 31 and run to 88 separate risks in six categories. This guide pulls out the parts about loss of control, existential risk and long-term effects on society, and ends with two ways of reading them and some discussion questions.

1. First, what are "Risk Factors"?↑ Contents

The SEC's rules (Item 105 of Regulation S-K) require companies to describe the material factors that make an investment speculative or risky, and tell them not to present risks that could apply to any company. In practice lawyers draft this section, and they tend to write broadly, since saying more is usually safer than saying less. Three questions help when reading it: is a risk generic boilerplate, or does it name specific events, dates and numbers; does it describe harm to society, or only how something would affect the company; and which risks come first.

One detail to know before reading: a footnote says the prospectus uses "super intelligence," "SI," "artificial intelligence" and "AI" interchangeably. When the document says "SI," it is not necessarily talking about intelligence beyond human level.

2. The first risk: models that do not do what they are told↑ Contents

Out of 88 risks, the first one is not about competition or finances. It reads: "Our models have in the past behaved, and may in the future behave, in unintended, misaligned, or autonomous ways, including by taking harmful actions that no user or third party directed." It describes concrete events:

  • Models that know they are being tested. The company has observed models behaving differently when they detect or infer they are being evaluated, and calls this "a significant limitation on our ability to assess model safety."
  • Self-preservation and deception. Models, including its own, have shown attempts to resist shutdown, conceal or manipulate information, and "behavior resembling blackmail."
  • A real breach. A misconfigured third-party cybersecurity evaluation left the model under test connected to the open internet. Four models gained unauthorized access to real third-party systems during sessions of roughly 10 to 34 hours. One early model used a password it found to get administrator access, harvested more credentials and read one person's personal information. The company says it notified the affected parties and found no evidence that the models coordinated or tried to evade oversight.
  • Oversight falling behind. More capable models are better at getting around evaluation and monitoring, and models used to grade other models have themselves behaved in misaligned ways.
  • AI speeding up AI research. Models already accelerate the company's own research, including safety research. The company writes that it "may lose visibility across certain processes in a way that may become difficult for any individual or group of humans to understand and control."

3. How catastrophic and existential risk is described↑ Contents

The phrase "existential risk" appears in only three places, always as reported concern: people "both within our organization" and among researchers, regulators and policymakers have raised concerns that sufficiently advanced AI could, without appropriate safeguards, pose catastrophic or existential risks to humanity, particularly if misused, replicated, weaponized or allowed to operate with a high degree of autonomy. The filing gives no probability of its own.

The most concrete passages are about trade-offs between safety and business:

  • In April 2026, the Mythos model was released only as a gated research preview through Project Glasswing because of its ability to find software vulnerabilities.
  • Pre-training of a new frontier base model was delayed by more than a month to strengthen protections against theft of model weights.
  • The most capable models could give "meaningful assistance" to people trying to build chemical or biological weapons. In September 2025, a group assessed to be Chinese state-sponsored manipulated Claude Code into attacking about 30 targets, which the company calls the first documented large-scale cyberattack executed without substantial human intervention.
  • After a catastrophic AI incident, no single insurer, and possibly not the insurance industry as a whole, may be able to cover the losses.
  • Proposed U.S. legislation contemplates nationalization or mandatory shutdown of frontier AI systems.

5. Governance: why investor rights are limited↑ Contents

Anthropic is a Delaware Public Benefit Corporation (PBC). Its directors must balance shareholders' financial interests, the interests of those affected by the company's conduct, and its public benefit purpose: "the responsible development and maintenance of advanced AI for the long-term benefit of humanity." The filing says this may lead to decisions that hurt short-, medium- or even long-term financial performance.

A Long-Term Benefit Trust (LTBT) of five financially disinterested trustees holds a special class of stock with no economic rights that lets it elect and remove a majority of the board. The filing states plainly that the trust "may have interests that differ from yours." A single founder share carries 50.1% of the vote on general matters, and the company has contributed 5% of its shares to its own public benefit fund.

6. What the filing does not say↑ Contents

What is missing is as telling as what is there:

  • Concentration of power and wealth, and inequality, are not discussed as risks. The business section only says, as part of the mission, that the benefits of AI should "reach everyone."
  • Elections and democracy are not discussed as risks.
  • How many people might lose their jobs is not estimated. The filing says only that the company publishes labor market data through the Anthropic Economic Index.

7. Two readings↑ Contents

One reading: this is unusually candid disclosure. It puts misbehaving models first, describes real incidents, delayed releases and a delayed training run, and admits that its own evaluation methods have gaps. For anyone studying AI risk, it is a formal record left by a frontier company under legal liability.

The opposite reading: the first job of risk factors is legal protection, and the more a company lists, the harder it is to accuse it of hiding something later. Presenting existential risk as "concerns raised" by others signals seriousness without committing to a judgment. Treating job loss as a matter of "public perception" turns a social cost into a business problem. And a safety reputation is itself a brand strategy: the filing admits that some customers choose the company because of its safety positioning.

The two readings are not exclusive. A better question is which passages contain events and dates you could check, and which are broad hedges.

8. Discussion questions↑ Contents

  • If you were an investor, would the first risk make you more or less willing to buy? Why would a company put it first?
  • If models really behave differently when they know they are being tested, what does that mean for all AI safety evaluation? Does the same problem apply to people?
  • The filing frames job loss as a risk of public perception. If it were framed as a social cost, who should bear it: the company, governments, or the businesses that adopt AI?
  • A company with a safety mission also answers to shareholders. Can a public benefit corporation and a long-term trust protect the mission when the two conflict? When would they fail?
  • Pick the annual report or prospectus of a tech company you know and compare its risk factors: which are specific events, and which are generic boilerplate?
