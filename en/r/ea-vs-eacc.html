Effective Altruism vs. e/acc: Trends, Debates and Philosophical Questions in the Age of AI

When people debate how fast AI should move and who should govern it, Silicon Valley and the policy world are often drawn as two camps. On one side is an AI-safety camp rooted in Effective Altruism (EA), worried that AI could slip out of human control. On the other is effective accelerationism (e/acc), which holds that technological progress is itself a moral duty and that slowing down is the real danger. This guide covers where the two camps came from, what has happened in recent years, what the arguments are really about, and several philosophical questions worth discussing in class. A list of key texts, critiques and middle-ground papers follows at the end.

1. Where the two camps came from↑ Contents

Effective Altruism is often traced to Peter Singer's 1972 essay: if we can prevent something bad without sacrificing anything of comparable moral importance, we ought to do it. Around 2009, Oxford philosophers Toby Ord and William MacAskill turned the idea into a movement that uses evidence and calculation to decide where money and time should go, with early attention on global poverty and animal welfare. Over time, many in the movement moved toward longtermism: far more people may live in the future than are alive now, so reducing the risk of human extinction has the highest expected value. Nick Bostrom, Ord's The Precipice, and Greaves and MacAskill's paper on strong longtermism all list misaligned AI among this century's largest existential risks. Through foundation funding, research institutes and personal networks, this line of thought has deeply shaped today's AI safety research.

Effective accelerationism emerged on Twitter in 2022 as an online movement whose very name mocks EA. Its pseudonymous founder Beff Jezos (later identified by journalists as physicist Guillaume Verdon) dressed the idea in thermodynamics: the universe tends to dissipate energy ever more efficiently, life, capital and technology extend that process, so accelerating along with it is "right." Its intellectual roots reach back to British philosopher Nick Land's accelerationism of the 1990s. In October 2023, venture capitalist Marc Andreessen published "The Techno-Optimist Manifesto," naming "existential risk," "trust and safety" and "tech ethics" among its enemies, and e/acc went from internet meme to a position with capital behind it.

2. A short timeline↑ Contents

  • November 2022: FTX, an exchange closely tied to EA, collapsed; its founder Sam Bankman-Fried was later convicted of fraud. EA's reputation and AI safety funding both took heavy damage.
  • March and May 2023: first an open letter calling for a six-month pause on training large models, then a one-sentence statement from the Center for AI Safety placing AI extinction risk alongside pandemics and nuclear war, signed by the leaders of OpenAI, Google DeepMind and Anthropic.
  • November 2023: OpenAI's board fired CEO Sam Altman, who returned within days; several board members seen as linked to EA circles left. The episode was widely read as the safety camp losing to the accelerationists.
  • November 2023: Ethereum co-founder Vitalik Buterin proposed d/acc (defensive accelerationism) as a third path between the two.
  • 2024: California's frontier AI safety bill SB 1047 was vetoed; Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei published "Machines of Loving Grace," describing in hopeful terms what powerful AI could achieve.
  • 2025: the International AI Safety Report chaired by Yoshua Bengio was released; in April two influential texts pointing in opposite directions appeared, the AI 2027 scenario, which imagines superintelligence arriving around 2027, and Narayanan and Kapoor's essay arguing that AI is a "normal technology"; in July the White House released an AI Action Plan centered on deregulation and winning the race; in September Yudkowsky and Soares published If Anyone Builds It, Everyone Dies; in October the Future of Life Institute launched a statement calling for a prohibition on developing superintelligence.

3. Five things the debate is really about↑ Contents

1. How big is the risk, and who gets to say? The safety camp often speaks of "p(doom)," the probability that AI destroys humanity, but these numbers are mostly personal judgments. Carlsmith raised his estimate for power-seeking AI from 5% to over 10%, while other forecasters give much lower figures. e/acc argues that such estimates have no empirical basis and amount to imagined doom used to win regulatory power.

2. Slowing down or speeding up: which costs more? e/acc's core argument is that delaying technology delays new medicines and economic growth, and today's patients and poor bear the cost. Interestingly, Bostrom's 2003 paper "Astronomical Waste" also grants that delay is enormously costly, yet reaches the opposite conclusion: because the future is so long, safety matters far more than speed. Both sides run the same kind of expected-value calculation; they differ in how they estimate the risk.

3. Should power be concentrated or spread out? The safety camp tends to call for licensing, compute controls and international coordination; critics say this would leave the strongest models in the hands of a few large companies and governments, or invite regulatory capture. e/acc favors open source and market competition, yet it too is driven by a small group of investors. Buterin's observation is that both sides have too many plans that put extreme power in very few hands.

4. Future risks or present harms? AI ethics scholars such as Timnit Gebru and Émile Torres argue that EA and e/acc are two branches of the same "TESCREAL" bundle of ideas, both chasing a utopia delivered by superintelligence while pushing aside harms already happening, such as bias, labor exploitation and surveillance. Atoosa Kasirzadeh proposes "accumulative" risk: existential risk need not come from a sudden loss of control one day; it can also build up from many small harms, which opens room for the two concerns to talk to each other.

5. Money and trust. Much of EA's funding once came from FTX, while e/acc's voice overlaps with venture capital interests. Each side accuses the other of talking about humanity's future while pursuing its own interests, which makes it hard for ordinary users to know whom to believe.

4. The philosophical questions underneath↑ Contents

  • Do future people count? Longtermism weighs people not yet born equally with people alive today. Do we have duties to people who do not yet exist? If so, should those duties be discounted?
  • Can a tiny probability times an enormous value justify action? If the stakes are large enough, any small probability can outweigh everything else, a problem known as "Pascal's mugging." Where does expected-value reasoning break down?
  • Can the ends justify the means? FTX led critics to ask whether maximizing good outcomes makes it easy to rationalize wrongful means. Srinivasan and Crary also criticize EA for measuring the efficiency of donations while ignoring the institutions that produce inequality.
  • Who defines "progress"? e/acc moves from "the universe tends to evolve this way" to "we ought to accelerate," a leap from is to ought. If humans are not the endpoint of that process, should we still care about humans?
  • Under uncertainty, be cautious or let go? The precautionary principle asks for proof of safety before deployment; the innovation principle says deploy first and correct later. For a technology that cannot be fully tested in advance, which burden of proof is more reasonable?

5. Positions between the two poles↑ Contents

Reducing the discussion to EA versus e/acc leaves out many other positions. d/acc favors acceleration but prioritizes defensive, decentralized and biosecurity technologies; the "AI as normal technology" view expects AI's effects to spread slowly, like electricity, and puts policy weight on resilience rather than on preventing superintelligence; AI ethics and fairness research asks who is harmed by algorithms and who can be held accountable. Many people working on AI safety do not identify with EA, and e/acc is more an internet culture than an organized movement. When you meet these two labels, it helps to ask first: who is speaking, where the money comes from, and whom they are asking to bear the risk.

6. Questions for discussion↑ Contents

  • What evidence would you use to convince someone that AI risk is large, or that it is exaggerated? Which kinds of evidence would you yourself not accept?
  • If a technology could save many lives but carries a small chance of disaster, whom would you trust to decide whether it is released?
  • Many countries, including Taiwan, are mainly users of AI and suppliers of chips rather than developers of frontier models. How does this debate relate to their situation?

7. References and further reading↑ Contents

Effective altruism and longtermism10

  • Singer, P. (1972). Famine, affluence, and morality. Philosophy & Public Affairs, 1(3), 229–243. ↗EA's philosophical starting point: if you can prevent something bad without sacrificing anything comparable, you ought to.
  • Bostrom, N. (2003). Astronomical waste: The opportunity cost of delayed technological development. Utilitas, 15(3), 308–314. ↗Delay is costly, but safety matters more than speed; an argument both camps cite.
  • Bostrom, N. (2019). The vulnerable world hypothesis. Global Policy, 10(4), 455–476. ↗Some technologies may destroy civilization by default without extraordinary governance; a key basis for the regulatory camp.
  • Greaves, H., & MacAskill, W. (2021). The case for strong longtermism. GPI Working Paper No. 5-2021. ↗The fullest philosophical defense of strong longtermism, with replies to objections such as cluelessness about the far future.
  • Ord, T. (2020). The precipice: Existential risk and the future of humanity. Hachette. ↗Estimates misaligned AI as one of this century's largest sources of existential risk.
  • Carlsmith, J. (2022). Is power-seeking AI an existential risk? arXiv:2206.13353. ↗Breaks AI existential risk into six premises with probability estimates; a frequently cited report in p(doom) debates.
  • Center for AI Safety. (2023). Statement on AI risk. ↗One sentence: mitigating AI extinction risk should be a global priority alongside pandemics and nuclear war.
  • Yudkowsky, E., & Soares, N. (2025). If anyone builds it, everyone dies. Little, Brown. ↗The strongest pessimistic case: superhuman AI would almost certainly escape control; argues for an international ban.
  • Kokotajlo, D., Alexander, S., Larsen, T., Lifland, E., & Dean, R. (2025). AI 2027. AI Futures Project. ↗A month-by-month scenario of superintelligence arriving, with "slowdown" and "race" endings.
  • Future of Life Institute. (2025). Statement on superintelligence. ↗Calls for a prohibition on developing superintelligence until there is scientific consensus and public buy-in.

Effective accelerationism and techno-optimism5

  • Beff Jezos & bayeslord. (2022). Notes on e/acc principles and tenets. ↗The founding e/acc text, using thermodynamics and "technocapital" to argue for acceleration.
  • Andreessen, M. (2023). The techno-optimist manifesto. Andreessen Horowitz. ↗Names existential risk, trust and safety, and tech ethics as enemies, bringing e/acc into the mainstream.
  • Amodei, D. (2024). Machines of loving grace. ↗An AI lab CEO on powerful AI's upside for health, economy and governance; an optimistic view from inside the safety camp.
  • Aschenbrenner, L. (2024). Situational awareness: The decade ahead. ↗Predicts AGI soon and frames it as a US–China national security race.
  • The White House. (2025). Winning the race: America's AI action plan. ↗The official document marking the US policy shift toward deregulation and the race.

Critiques and middle positions8

  • Gebru, T., & Torres, É. P. (2024). The TESCREAL bundle: Eugenics and the promise of utopia through artificial general intelligence. First Monday, 29(4). ↗Argues that EA, longtermism and accelerationism share eugenic roots and neglect present harms.
  • Srinivasan, A. (2015). Stop the robot apocalypse. London Review of Books, 37(18). ↗An early critique of EA: it measures giving efficiency without asking what causes inequality.
  • Crary, A. (2021). Against "effective altruism." Radical Philosophy, 2.10, 33–43. ↗Critiques EA's moral outlook as too narrow, on both philosophical and institutional grounds.
  • Buterin, V. (2023). My techno-optimism. ↗Proposes d/acc: accelerate, but choose directions, favoring defensive and decentralized technologies.
  • Narayanan, A., & Kapoor, S. (2025). AI as normal technology. Knight First Amendment Institute. ↗Treats AI not as a coming superintelligence but as a technology that diffuses slowly, like electricity.
  • Kasirzadeh, A. (2025). Two types of AI existential risk: Decisive and accumulative. Philosophical Studies, 182(7), 1975–2003. ↗Distinguishes sudden "decisive" from "accumulative" existential risk, bridging safety and ethics concerns.
  • Bengio, Y., et al. (2025). International AI safety report. ↗About 100 experts review the scientific evidence on general-purpose AI risks, aiming not to take sides.
  • Wikipedia. Effective accelerationism. ↗A quick entry point to e/acc's people, events and controversies.
